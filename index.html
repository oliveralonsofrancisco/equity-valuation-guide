Topic 6 · Part 1 · Section 1 · Basic ideas

Intrinsic value vs market price

Valuation estimates the theoretically correct (intrinsic or fair) value of a company, investment or asset, as opposed to its cost or current market value. If the two differ, the market has mispriced the security.

stakeholder vs shareholder5 reasons to value3 model families

Stakeholders and shareholders

A stakeholder is any entity (individual, group, organization or government) with a vested interest in the success of an organization and affected directly or indirectly by its performance.

A shareholder owns at least one share of the joint-stock company and has a financial stake in its success.

All shareholders are stakeholders, but not all stakeholders are shareholders.

Why do we value assets?

  • Buying or selling a business: fair transaction price.
  • Strategic planning: invest only in projects that raise NPV; every investment decision is a mini-valuation.
  • Capital financing: lenders assess the ability to generate cash flows and service debt.
  • Securities investing: is the stock or bond priced in line with intrinsic value and comparables?
  • Tax and financial reporting: fair value for compliance, estate planning, reporting.

The three families of equity valuation models

1 · Present value models
Discounted models (dividends, cash flows). Intrinsic value of the business on its own. In this course: only DDM.
2 · Comparable company analysis
"Comps": ratios of similar listed companies applied to the target. A relative valuation.
3 · Asset-based models
Fair market value of assets minus liabilities. Capital-intensive firms or liquidation scenarios.

Topic 6 · Part 1 · Section 2 · Comparable company analysis

Peers → multiples → value

Trading comps infer value from what similar listed companies trade for. "10× EBITDA" means the market pays €10 for every €1 of annual EBITDA. A market-based value is not necessarily the correct fundamental value.

Step 1 · Choosing peers

The most judgment-intensive step. Match on industry, geography (market, currency, regulation), size, growth and profitability.

Retailer A

Fast expansion · strong online sales · high margins · limited debt

Retailer B

Slow sales growth · weak profitability · high store rents · higher operating risk
Two firms in the same industry need not deserve the same multiple.

Step 2 · Two company values

Equity value (market cap) = shares × price. Example: 1m × €20 = €20m.

Enterprise value ≈ value of the operating business before splitting it between lenders and shareholders.

€20m + €8m − €3m = €25m. Market prices refer to a date; financial metrics refer to a period (LTM or forward).

Match the multiple to the claim

Enterprise-value multiples

EV/Revenue · EV/EBITDA · EV/EBIT
Pre-financing operating metrics → give EV. Subtract net debt to reach equity.

Equity-value multiples

P/E (Equity value / Net income) · P/Book
Metrics attributable to equity holders → give equity value or price directly.
Never multiply EBITDA by a P/E, or net income by an EV/EBITDA.

EBITDA = earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization. An operating earnings measure, not a cash flow: it ignores capex, working-capital investment and taxes.

Mean or median?

Mean

Sum ÷ number of peers. Sensitive to extreme values.

Median

Middle value after sorting. More robust with an extreme multiple.
Never delete an outlier just to get the value you wanted.

A lower EV/EBITDA can reflect slower growth, lower margins, heavier investment needs or higher risk. A low multiple does not mean the stock is cheap.

Step 5 · Value the target with EV/EBITDAslide 25
Target: EBITDA €50m · peer median 10× · net debt €120m · diluted shares 20m.
  1. EV = 10 × €50m = €500m (apply the peer multiple to target EBITDA).
  2. Equity value = €500m − €120m = €380m (net debt = debt − cash, so cash is already inside).
  3. Price = €380m ÷ 20m = €19.00 per share.
Alternative: forward P/Eslides 26–27
P/E = price ÷ EPS. Company A: €30 / €2 = 15×, investors pay €15 for each €1 of annual EPS (not a guaranteed 15-year payback). Peers trade at 12×, 14×, 16×; target expected EPS next year = €1.20.
  1. Median peer P/E = 14×.
  2. Price = 14 × €1.20 = €16.80. P/E gives the price directly (equity multiple). Match forward P/E with forecast EPS.

Why €19.00 vs €16.80?

Not automatically an error. The two multiples rely on different measures (depreciation, interest, taxes). Check growth, leverage, tax rates, margins, capital intensity and accounting policies.

Comps often produce a range, not one indisputable price.

Low P/E may mean

slower expected growth · higher business or financial risk · maybe undervalued

High P/E may mean

strong expected growth · low risk / high quality · maybe too expensive

Common student mistakes

MismatchP/E with EBITDA; EV/EBITDA with earnings
Datesprices and financials from inconsistent periods
Peersinappropriate businesses or risk profiles
Accountingunadjusted exceptional items, inconsistent EBITDA
Interpretationtreating the median as an objectively true value

Where comps are used: equity research, IPO pricing, M&A advisory, cross-checking DCF terminal-value multiples.

Topic 6 · Part 1 · Section 3 · Asset-based valuation models

What it owns − what it owes

Intrinsic value of a common share from the estimated value of the assets minus the estimated value of liabilities and preferred shares. Book (carrying) values are adjusted to estimated current values.

With 1m ordinary shares: €4.5m ÷ 1m = €4.50 per share. This is an estimated asset-based value, not necessarily the observed market price.

IAS 16: two ways to carry PP&E

Cost model (IAS 16.30)

Cost − accumulated depreciation − impairment. A rise in land value does not raise the carrying amount.

Revaluation model (IAS 16.31)

Fair value at revaluation − later depreciation and impairment. Revalue often enough to stay close to fair value.

IAS 16.29: the policy is chosen per asset class, not per asset.

RouteReported equityAdjustmentsAdj. NAV

Both routes give the same adjusted NAV; only the adjustment needed differs. The accounting model changes reported equity, not the assets.

Useful for

  • Real estate companies (land, buildings)
  • Investment holding companies (financial assets)
  • Companies in liquidation
  • Asset-intensive businesses

Limitations

  • Brands and customer relationships are hard to value
  • Future earnings and growth may not be captured
  • Some assets have no observable market value
  • Different models give different estimates
Topic 6 · Part 1 · Section 4 · Equity distributions

Cash, stock, scrip and rights

Companies pay regular cash dividends, but also special dividends, stock dividends, scrip (flexible) dividends, share buybacks and returns of contributed capital. The standard DDM only discounts expected cash dividends per share.

Cash leaves the company, so the theoretical price falls by €1 per share, all else equal.

Not all distributions are dividends: BME 2025

Ordinary cash dividend
Charged to profits or distributable reserves.
Share premium repayment
1m shares issued at €5 with €1 nominal → capital €1m + premium €4m. Repaying €1m from premium is a return of contributed equity.
Capital (nominal) reduction
Nominal €10 → €8 on 1m shares: cash returned = (10 − 8) × 1m = €2m. Shares and ownership % unchanged.
All three transfer cash, but their legal and accounting origins differ. Treat returns of capital separately; do not add them mechanically to regular DPS.

Cash vs stock dividend

Cash dividend: money leaves the company. Stock dividend: the same equity value is split into more shares. Ownership: 100/1,000 = 10% before and 110/1,100 = 10% after.

Scrip / flexible dividend (dividendo flexible)

Each existing share gets one free allocation right (derecho de asignación gratuita). A right is not a share: a set number of rights is needed per new share.

Which dividends enter the DDM?

Included

Regular expected cash dividends. Special or irregular cash dividends in a multi-period DDM. = expected cash dividend per share at time t.

Not a separate cash flow

Extra shares from a stock dividend (no cash paid). Account consistently for the share count and future DPS. Flexible programmes need careful cash-vs-share treatment.
Topic 6 · Part 2 · Dividend discount model

Discount the dividends

The DDM values a share as the present value of the cash dividends expected, plus any selling price. The discount rate is the cost of equity , usually from CAPM.

one-periodmulti-yearGordonmultistageCAPM β

CAPM and beta

β measures sensitivity to market returns (systematic risk), not total volatility. β = 1.5: the stock tends to move 1.5 pp for a 1 pp market move. β = 1: expected return equals the market return. is the equity risk premium.

Beta is an estimate, not directly observed. Bloomberg, Yahoo Finance and Morningstar can report different betas for the same stock (different periods, frequency, index, adjustments) without any being wrong.

Gordon growth model

  • Dividend growth is constant and perpetual.
  • Required return is constant.
  • Requires g < k.

, payout = DPS / EPS. ROE measures how effectively retained earnings generate additional earnings. The formula is the sum of a geometric series.

Unsuitable when the company pays no dividend, is young and entering its growth phase, or has growth expected to change toward its long-term rate.

Topic 7 · Part 1 · Portfolio risk and return

Diversify: risk is more than the average

Last year's winning asset class rarely repeats (REITs +50.5% in 2021, −20.2% in 2022). A portfolio aims to be less dependent on forecasting the single best performer. Its risk depends on how the assets move together.

σ and variancecovariance / ρopportunity setefficient frontiersystematic risk

Dow 30, monthly returns Jan 2009–Dec 2013: same 30 stocks, same 1.69% average return.

Two benefits of combining securities

  1. Avoid a disaster from overinvesting in one asset. $10,000 fully in Reddit in 2026 (to 6 Nov): −58.63% → $4,137.
  2. Substantially reduce risk, measured by variance or standard deviation.

A portfolio: €10,000 as €5,000 Stock A, €3,000 Stock B, €2,000 bonds → weights 50%, 30%, 20% (sum 100%).

Measuring risk

Variance measures dispersion around the mean (volatility). σ is in the same units as returns. A wider, flatter density (variance 100) is riskier than a narrow, peaked one (variance 10). VaR is another measure (CFA Level II).

Portfolio mean and variance (N assets)

Weights can be negative but must sum to 1.
Covariance: do returns move together (+) or inversely (−)?
Correlation , from −1 to +1.
As N grows, covariance terms grow disproportionately versus variance terms. What matters when adding a stock is its correlation with what you already hold.

Two-asset opportunity set

Extreme correlations

ρ = +1: . A straight line; no diversification benefit.

ρ < +1: . The set bends to the left.

ρ = −1: . Zero risk is reachable with .

Holding all else constant (positive weights), higher correlation → more volatile portfolio; lower correlation → less volatile.

Diversification ratio = weighted average of individual σ ÷ portfolio σ.

Efficient frontier

MVF: minimum variance for each expected return. GMV (D): lowest-risk portfolio on the MVF. Markowitz efficient frontier: the MVF above the GMV. No risk-averse investor holds a portfolio to the right of the MVF.

Adding an asset that is not perfectly correlated pushes the opportunity set further to the northwest.

Limits of diversification

Firm-specific risk

idiosyncratic · unsystematic · diversifiable
Product recall, lawsuit, disappointing earnings, loss of a key customer. Shrinks as 1/N.

Market risk

systematic · common · not diversifiable
Interest-rate rise, recession, inflation shock, global crisis. The risk floor that remains.

Reading the curve

  • Falling part: idiosyncratic risk diluted by more holdings.
  • Horizontal asymptote: common exposure remains.
  • Much of the benefit came with about 25–30 stocks in the illustration; not a universal threshold.
  • Higher ρ → higher risk floor. If markets fail totally, diversification is a false promise.

Crypto? Ask how a small allocation changes the risk of the whole portfolio: standalone volatility, correlation (also in sell-offs), weight.
